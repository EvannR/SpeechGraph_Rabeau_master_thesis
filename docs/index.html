The concept

What is a speech graph?

Each word in a transcript becomes a node. Consecutive words are connected by a directed edge. When a word repeats, the edge loops back to the existing node — revealing the cyclical structure of speech.

Graph metrics

Three structural measures

Each verbatim produces a directed graph. Three metrics capture complementary aspects of narrative structure.

LSCC
Largest Strongly Connected Component
The biggest cluster of words mutually reachable by following directed edges — a proxy for narrative cohesion.
AD
Average Degree
Edges divided by nodes. Captures how densely connected the word transitions are — a measure of lexical connectivity.
ASPL
Average Shortest Path Length
Mean number of steps between any two nodes in the LSCC — reflects how tightly the speech network is organized.
Key results

What we found

119 French-speaking adults (50 SSD, 68 controls) each described 9 picture sequences across 3 difficulty levels. Linear mixed models revealed distinct group-level differences in graph structure.

Reduced LSCC in SSD

Individuals with SSD produced speech graphs with smaller strongly connected components, even after controlling for speech length.

Higher Average Degree in SSD

SSD speakers showed greater lexical repetition — more edges per node — suggesting narrower but more recycled vocabulary.

Shorter ASPL in SSD

Path lengths within the LSCC were shorter for SSD, reflecting a more tightly looped, less expansive narrative structure.

No Group × Level interaction

Group differences were consistent across all three difficulty levels — referential complexity did not amplify or reduce the SSD effect.
